Kids Who Game: Age, Aggression and Studies

Yourdost parent survey · December 2020 · 67 parents whose kids play online games

In brief

Kids aged 6–12 stand out: they play the most, about half play violent games, and their parents are the most worried about aggression. Parents' worry about studies depends less on the kids' age than on whether the parent has ever played games.

  • Aggression: 52% of parents of 6–12-year-olds have a high fear of aggressive behaviour, against 11–22% for other age groups.
  • Studies: a high fear of academic harm is common for 6–12s (60%) and adult children (65%), and lower for under-6s (22%) and teens (30%).
  • Violent games: these go with more fear of aggression, but not with more fear about studies.
  • Parents' own gaming: 74% of parents who never played have a high fear of academic harm, against 38% of parents who play with their kids.

What this survey can tell us: it asked parents what they fear and what they see. It did not measure how kids actually behave or what grades they get. So "aggression" and "academic performance" here mean parents' concern about them, not measured outcomes.

Violent games by age

About half of kids aged 6 and over play violent games, while almost no under-6s do.

Violent games here means the shooting and fighting games parents named: PUBG, Free Fire, Call of Duty, Counter-Strike, Fortnite, Overwatch, Halo, Mortal Kombat, Gangster and Brawlhalla.

Parents of 6–12-year-olds seem by far the most worried about aggression. For older kids, the worry appears mainly when the kids play violent games.

  • The gap between 6–12s and every other age group is the strongest pattern here, and unlikely to be chance (p ≈ 0.005).
  • For 6–12s, parents worry about aggression whatever the game: 54% with violent games, 50% without. This may reflect worry about the age itself, such as tantrums when screen time ends.
  • For teens and adult children, the only parents with a high fear of aggression are those whose kids play violent games. The groups are very small, so treat this as a hint.
  • Medium fear is common at every age: 83–89% of parents of under-6s, 6–12s and adults report high or medium fear. Teens' parents are the exception at 40%.

Worry about studies is high for 6–12s and adult children, and seems unrelated to whether the games are violent.

  • Violent and non-violent games carry about the same academic worry: 54% against 52%.
  • Parents of teens seem calmer, and 80% of them ban gaming during exams. A clear exam rule may give parents a sense of control.
  • Fewer than half of parents of 6–12s ban gaming during exams, even though 60% fear academic harm.
  • For adult children (often college students), the worry is likely about studies and careers rather than school marks.

Do their parents play?

Parents who play games with their kids worry much less about both aggression and studies. Playing together does not seem to keep kids away from violent games, except for the youngest.

  • The gap in fear between parents who never played and those who play with their kids is unlikely to be chance (aggression p ≈ 0.02, studies p ≈ 0.008).
  • Under-6s: 8 of 9 parents play with their kids, and none of those kids play violent games.
  • 6–12s: 7 of the 11 kids whose parents play with them still play violent games. Parents may be joining in on the same games rather than steering kids away.
  • Kids of parents who never played lean slightly more toward violent games (52% against 38%), but this gap could be chance.
  • One possible reading: parents who know games from the inside may judge the risks as smaller. The survey cannot show which comes first.

Limits of this data

These are patterns in a small survey of parents' views. They are leads to test, not proof that games cause aggression or poor grades.

  • Fears, not outcomes: the survey asked how much parents fear aggression and academic harm. It did not measure kids' behaviour or marks.
  • Small groups: 67 parents in total, and many cells hold 4–11 parents. Only three gaps here are unlikely to be chance: 6–12s against other ages on aggression fear, and parents who never played against those who play with their kids on both fears.
  • Age is the family's, not one kid's: parents picked one age group for their kids, and the games named may belong to any of them.
  • Games sorted by hand: violent games were picked from the names parents typed. 6 parents didn't know what their kids play.
  • Timing: December 2020, during the pandemic, when kids were home and gaming more.
  • No causes: this data shows which answers go together, not what causes what.
  • Source: Yourdost parent survey, 100 responses, December 2020.
